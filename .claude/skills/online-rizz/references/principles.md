# Principles

Evidence-backed principles for online dating messaging. Each is a default, not a
law — read the person and the thread. Sources in `sources.md`.

## 1. Personalize over generic
Messages that reference something specific in the person's profile or photos get
materially more replies than "hey" / "how's your day". Generic openers signal
copy-paste and low effort. **Always** find a concrete hook: a trip, a pet, a
book, a prompt answer, a job, a niche interest.
> Verified support: openers with more "you" and less "I" get more replies —
> personalization is recipient-focus, not flattery (Schöndienst & Dang-Xuan
> 2011). Effort spent on *length* doesn't pay off; relevance does (Bruch &
> Newman 2018).

## 2. Confidence + warmth, not cleverness
The goal isn't a viral one-liner. A warm, self-assured, genuinely curious
message beats a "perfect" scripted line. People respond to someone who seems
relaxed and interested, not someone performing.

## 3. Make the reply easy *and* interesting
Ask something that's low-effort to answer but opens a fun thread — a light
opinion, a playful either/or, a follow-up on their own hook. Avoid dead-end
yes/no questions and avoid heavy/interview questions early. Thoughtful questions
are the single most-supported lever: 85% are likelier to want a second date when
asked them (Hinge 2025). *(Note: replying faster/shorter is NOT a verified
lever — that tactic was refuted; see common-mistakes.md.)*

## 4. Match (and gently lift) their energy
Mirror message length, tone, and emoji use. If they send two lines, don't send
ten. If they're playful, play back. Calibrate up slowly — don't be the only one
escalating.

## 5. Specificity is attraction
Vague compliments ("you're gorgeous", "you seem cool") are forgettable and can
read as creepy on looks alone. Specific, observational, slightly playful
comments show you actually paid attention.

## 6. Self-disclosure, not interrogation
Trade questions for small reveals about yourself. A thread that's question →
question → question feels like an interview. Give them something to react to.

## 7. Banter builds tension; tension needs a release (the date)
Light teasing, callbacks to earlier jokes, and playful challenges build
attraction. One verified flirting move is "imagined togetherness" — playfully
constructing a shared-future image and keeping it open-ended (Mortensen 2017).
But banter has a shelf life — the point is to move to a real plan. (Humor helps
*conditionally*, not always — see common-mistakes.md.)

## 8. Move to a date before the chat goes stale
The biggest unforced error is chatting forever. Once there's genuine
back-and-forth and a hook to hang it on, propose a specific, low-pressure plan.
Endless texting kills more matches than a slightly-early ask-out.

## 9. Low pressure, high specificity on the ask-out
"We should grab coffee sometime" is weak. A concrete, easy-to-say-yes-to,
no-pressure suggestion ("there's a good coffee place near X — free Thursday?")
converts better and respects their autonomy.

## 10. Read disinterest and back off gracefully
Slow, short, low-effort replies or a clear no = back off. Persistence-after-no
is not rizz; it's harassment. Graceful exits protect your reputation and respect
the other person.

## 11. Honesty and consent are the whole game
The aim is to let the *real* you land with someone who'd actually like you — not
to manipulate. Tactics built on deception (negging, fake scarcity,
love-bombing) damage trust and tend to backfire, and we don't use them. The
evidence-informed canon (Logan Ury / Hinge Labs + the academic literature) is
itself consent-respecting; the best-supported lever — genuine curiosity — is
inherently non-manipulative.
